# Technology Order of Battle — Counting Capabilities In Technology Races

When two or more actors race to dominate a technology, the outcome depends on their respective capabilities — not on narrative claims about who's "winning." Capability has structural components that can be measured: compute capacity, talent concentration, data access, capital availability, intellectual property position, manufacturing capability, regulatory environment.

This skill is the disciplined comparison. Modeled on military OOB but applied to technology races (US vs China in AI, US vs Europe in EVs, established players vs disruptors in any maturing technology). Whoever has structural capability advantage usually wins long-term, even if narrative shifts in short-term.

## Prerequisites

- Defined technology race (specific technology, defined competitors)
- Available capability data (varies by technology and accessibility)
- Time investment justified by stakes of the race

## Core principle

> Technology race outcomes depend on capability differentials, not narrative momentum. Narrative shifts; capability accumulates slowly. The actor with structural capability advantage usually wins long-term — but the analyst must measure capability, not narrative.

## The capability components

**1. Compute capacity**

For computational technologies, raw compute is foundational. Measured in:
- FLOPS available for relevant workloads
- Specialized hardware (GPUs, TPUs, ASICs)
- Total infrastructure scale
- Energy availability for compute scaling
- Cloud vs on-premise distribution

Critical for AI, simulation, scientific computing.

**2. Talent concentration**

Top-tier talent in the specific domain:
- PhD researchers in field
- Top-tier engineers with relevant experience
- Specialists from adjacent fields
- Educational pipeline producing new talent
- Retention rate of top talent

Critical for all advanced technologies. Talent is mobile but slow-moving.

**3. Data access**

For data-driven technologies:
- Volume of relevant data
- Quality and labeling
- Diversity (representation across cases)
- Continuous renewal
- Legal/regulatory access rights
- Data moats (proprietary access)

Critical for AI, ML applications, drug discovery.

**4. Capital resources**

Available investment:
- Government R&D budgets
- Corporate R&D spending
- VC and private investment
- Public market access for funding
- Time horizon of capital (patient vs impatient)
- Capital efficiency (return per dollar invested)

Critical for capital-intensive technology development.

**5. Intellectual property position**

Patent and trade secret position:
- Patent portfolio in field
- Quality and breadth of patents
- Trade secret protection
- Cross-licensing relationships
- Litigation capability

Critical for technologies where IP creates defensive moats.

**6. Manufacturing capability**

For physical technologies:
- Production capacity
- Quality and yield
- Supply chain integration
- Tooling and equipment
- Workforce skill
- Geographic distribution

Critical for hardware, biotech, materials science.

**7. Regulatory environment**

How regulation affects each actor:
- Permissive vs restrictive home regulations
- Export control limitations
- Intellectual property protection
- Antitrust restrictions
- International market access

Critical when regulation differs substantially between competitors.

**8. Time-to-market**

Speed of moving from research to commercial deployment:
- Decision-making speed
- Bureaucratic agility
- Iteration cycles
- Customer feedback integration
- Risk tolerance

Critical for all races; especially decisive in fast-moving fields.

## The procedure

### Step 1 — Define race and competitors

Specific technology and specific competitors (named actors, not abstract categories). For US-China AI: named labs, named companies, named institutions on each side.

### Step 2 — Score each component for each competitor

For each of eight components, score each competitor on 1-10 scale with brief justification.

Where data is uncertain, use ranges (e.g., "6-8" rather than fake precision).

### Step 3 — Compute aggregate capability scores

Simple average across components, or weighted if some components matter more for specific technology. Document weighting rationale if not equal.

### Step 4 — Identify capability differentials

Where competitors' capabilities differ most:
- Which competitor leads in which components?
- What's the magnitude of leads?
- Are leads narrowing or widening?

Largest differentials in highest-weight components most predict outcomes.

### Step 5 — Identify capability dynamics

Capabilities aren't static. Project trajectory:
- Compute: which side scaling faster?
- Talent: which side attracting/retaining?
- Capital: which side has more sustained funding?
- Etc.

The race is over time horizons; trajectories matter as much as current state.

### Step 6 — Identify structural constraints

Some competitors have structural ceilings:
- Export controls limiting compute access
- Population dynamics limiting talent pool
- Regulatory environments limiting deployment
- Capital cycles affecting investment

These constraints persist even if motivation increases.

### Step 7 — Forecast outcomes

Based on current capability scores, trajectories, and constraints, forecast race outcome:
- Who wins in 1, 3, 5, 10 years?
- What scenarios produce different outcomes?
- What disrupting events would change forecast?

## Worked example — US vs China in AI (illustrative, May 2026)

**Race:** Frontier AI model development

**Competitors:**
- US: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Microsoft, Meta, plus academic-government complex
- China: DeepSeek, Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent, plus government-academic complex

**Capability scoring:**

**Compute capacity:**
- US: 9 (Nvidia GPU access, hyperscaler infrastructure, sustained scaling)
- China: 6 (export controls limiting Nvidia access; domestic chips improving but lag)
- Differential: large US advantage; structural due to export controls

**Talent:**
- US: 9 (top researchers concentrated; international talent attraction)
- China: 7 (large talent base; some emigration concerns; strong in specific subfields)
- Differential: moderate US advantage; narrowing as China retains talent

**Data:**
- US: 8 (broad internet data access; English language advantage)
- China: 8 (Chinese internet ecosystem; specific government data access)
- Differential: roughly equal in different domains

**Capital:**
- US: 9 (massive private investment; public market access)
- China: 7 (government direction; some private; capital controls limiting)
- Differential: US advantage in flexibility

**Intellectual property:**
- US: 8 (patent leadership; some trade secret protection)
- China: 6 (catching up; some IP enforcement issues)
- Differential: moderate US advantage

**Manufacturing capability:**
- US: 5 (limited domestic chip manufacturing; reliance on TSMC)
- China: 6 (improving domestic; Huawei chip programs)
- Differential: roughly equal weakness for both vs Taiwan; tactical advantage to China

**Regulatory environment:**
- US: 6 (some restrictions emerging; mostly permissive)
- China: 4 (significant content restrictions; censorship requirements)
- Differential: US advantage

**Time-to-market:**
- US: 8 (rapid iteration; private companies move fast)
- China: 7 (faster than typical Chinese tech; some government coordination delays)
- Differential: small US advantage

**Aggregate scores:**
- US: 7.75
- China: 6.375

**Capability differential:** moderate US advantage; not overwhelming.

**Trajectory:**
- Compute: US lead may widen if export controls effective, may narrow if domestic Chinese chips successful
- Talent: probably narrowing as China retains and US tightens immigration
- Other components: stable or slowly changing

**Structural constraints:**
- China: chip export controls binding; capital efficiency; censorship regime
- US: manufacturing dependence on Taiwan; regulatory uncertainty growing

**Forecast:**
- 1-year: US maintains modest lead in frontier capabilities
- 3-year: US lead persists but narrows; China catches up in specific applications
- 5-year: race may converge in some domains; specific Chinese strengths emerge in deployment-heavy applications; US may retain lead in foundational research
- 10-year: highly uncertain — depends on Taiwan situation, immigration policies, talent dynamics

**Most decisive variable:** Taiwan (chip manufacturing). Disruption or capture changes everything.

## Anti-patterns

- **Aggregate scores hiding component asymmetries.** Two competitors with same aggregate score but different component distributions can have very different prospects. Component-level analysis matters more than aggregate.
- **Static capability assessment.** Race plays out over time; trajectories matter as much as current state.
- **Ignoring structural constraints.** Some constraints can't be overcome by motivation or investment. Identify them.
- **Narrative-driven scoring.** Scoring based on what mainstream narrative says about who's leading. Score based on capabilities.
- **Single-decisive-variable thinking.** Most races have multiple components; declaring one component decisive often misses others.
- **Overestimating narrative leader.** Whoever has best PR isn't necessarily structural leader. Capability assessment cuts through PR.

## Integration with Лаборатория знаний

Used for major technology races where investment outcomes depend on understanding race dynamics.

**Updated:** quarterly for tracked races; immediately on major events (new entrants, capability breakthroughs, regulatory shifts).

**Triggers cross-references:** with ОСП for investment positioning, with `iw-watchlist` for race-altering events.

Stored in KnowledgeArtifact with separate race-specific records.

This skill, when applied to major races, prevents the common error of investing based on narrative leader rather than capability leader. Capability usually prevails; narrative shifts.
