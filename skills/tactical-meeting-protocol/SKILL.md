# Tactical Meeting Protocol — Performing The Briefing

A perfect briefing produces nothing if the owner can't deploy it tactically in the meeting. Real meetings have dynamics — power, time, attention, group dynamics, conversational flow. The owner has to navigate these while applying the analytical framework.

This skill is the discipline of in-meeting tactics. Not what to ask (that's combat-questions-design) but when, how, in what tone, and what to do with the answer.

## Prerequisites

- Combat questions designed (Phase 4 complete)
- Pre-meeting intel ideally completed (knows attendees)
- Owner has reviewed briefing
- Awareness of meeting context (formal vs informal, time available, dynamics)

## Core principle

> The meeting is a performance with multiple actors and dynamics beyond technical content. Information extraction depends not just on having the right questions but on tactical deployment — when to ask, how to phrase, when to push, when to retreat, when to listen. The owner who has good questions but poor tactics extracts less than the owner with mediocre questions and excellent tactics.

## The three meeting phases

### Phase A — Opening (first 5-10 minutes)

**Purpose:** establish framing, signal engagement, listen.

**Owner role:**
- Brief opening question that signals technical engagement without being adversarial
- Listen to their opening pitch (they've optimized this; learn from what they emphasize and what they skip)
- Note who actually presents vs who supports (often the substantive person isn't the one talking)

**Avoid:**
- Asking hardest question first (creates adversarial frame)
- Detailed technical questions before they've completed framing
- Showing your hand on what you know

**Useful opening signals:**
- Terminology used naturally (signals familiarity)
- Reference to specific recent development in field (signals current awareness)
- Question that requires them to engage their best technical depth (signals you'll evaluate substantively)

### Phase B — Probing (middle, longest portion)

**Purpose:** extract information through structured questioning.

**Owner role:**
- Deploy combat questions in tactical order
- Read responses against expected good/uncertain/evasive patterns
- Follow up when answers are incomplete
- Adjust subsequent questions based on what's been revealed

**Tactical patterns:**

**The "naive" question:**
Sometimes asking a question that sounds elementary forces them to explain in ways that reveal depth or shallowness. "Wait, can you explain how the basic process works again?" — their explanation reveals whether they understand fundamentals.

**The follow-up loop:**
When answer is non-specific: "That's helpful — can you give me a specific example?"
When answer is specific but limited: "Got it — what's the broader pattern?"
The loop continues until specific + general are both addressed.

**The redirect:**
When they pivot to comfortable territory: gently redirect. "That's interesting — let me come back to my original question..."

**The silence:**
After they finish answering, brief silence often elicits more. They feel pressure to fill the silence and may reveal more than initial answer.

**The framing question:**
Sometimes the question isn't about content but about position. "How do you think about this?" reveals their actual mental model.

**The third-party reference:**
"I was talking to [adjacent expert / competitor reference] who suggested asking about X..."
Frames question as informed third-party concern, harder to dismiss.

### Phase C — Closing (last 5-10 minutes)

**Purpose:** consolidate, signal next steps, leave constructively.

**Owner role:**
- Summarize what was learned (signals engagement and good faith)
- Surface the most important unanswered question
- Define follow-up if interested, or clarify if not
- Avoid revealing final assessment

**Useful closing patterns:**
- "What I take from this discussion is..." [framing-establishing summary]
- "The question I'd most want answered before further discussion is..." [signals what you need]
- "Thanks for the time" [neutral close, doesn't commit either way]

## Reading body language and conversational dynamics

Some signals worth attending:

### Confidence signals (when answering technical questions):
- **Genuine:** uses specific examples, acknowledges uncertainty, references concrete data
- **Performed:** strong assertions without specifics, dismissive of alternatives, fluent rather than careful

### Evasion signals:
- Pivot phrases ("Great question, what's really important is...")
- Long preambles delaying answer
- Returning to memorized talking points
- Looking at others before answering (seeking permission/coordination)
- Sudden technicality designed to obscure

### Group dynamics:
- Who interrupts whom (revealed hierarchy)
- Whose answers others defer to (real expertise location)
- Who answers what (specialization vs generalist coverage)
- Side glances during certain questions (uncertainty/coordination)
- Visible discomfort with specific topics (sensitivity)

### Recovery patterns:
When they realize an answer wasn't strong:
- Fast pivot to next topic (signals they recognize weakness)
- Doubling down with more confident assertions (signals defensiveness)
- Appeal to authority ("our advisor [X] thinks this is solved")
- Promise of future evidence ("this will be addressed in our next phase")

## Tactical scenarios

### Scenario: They provide too-strong answer (better than expected)

Possibilities:
- Answer is real (project is stronger than briefing suggested)
- Answer is rehearsed but accurate
- Answer is incorrectly summarizing reality

Response: probe with follow-up that requires application of the answer to specific case. If they can apply it specifically, more likely real.

### Scenario: They deflect a key question

Tactical options:
- Repeat question more specifically
- Rephrase same question with different framing
- Save for end and return to it ("I want to come back to my earlier question about X...")
- Note the deflection and move on (signal they may not have answer)

### Scenario: They reveal something unexpected

Possibilities:
- New positive information (project stronger than expected)
- New negative information (problem revealed)
- They didn't realize they revealed it

Response: stay neutral, don't reveal that this is significant, note carefully, follow up to confirm.

### Scenario: Conflict among their team

When team members disagree visibly during meeting:
- This is high-information moment
- Don't try to resolve their disagreement
- Note who took which position
- Follow up later asking for clarification (forces them to address the disagreement)

### Scenario: Time pressure (running short)

Prioritize:
- Top 1-2 critical failure mode questions (must have)
- Marker question (if not yet asked)
- Skip nice-to-have, comparison questions

### Scenario: Owner getting overwhelmed

Pause tactics:
- "Let me think about that..."
- "Can you give me a moment to digest this?"
- Refer to notes/briefing
- Take notes openly (signals engagement, buys time)

## The procedure for using this skill

### Step 1 — Read briefing thoroughly before meeting

Owner should know combat questions, expected response patterns, top failure modes well enough to deploy without consulting paper continuously.

### Step 2 — Establish meeting style

Adapt based on context:
- Formal academic/government setting: more measured, longer turn-taking
- Informal startup pitch: more interactive, faster turn-taking
- Multi-party investor meeting: more strategic timing
- One-on-one: more personal, deeper

### Step 3 — Execute three phases

Opening → Probing → Closing per protocol above.

### Step 4 — Track responses against expectations

For each combat question, was response:
- Good (matches expected good answer)
- Uncertain (matches expected uncertain)
- Evasive (matches expected evasive)
- Unexpected (better or worse than expected)

This determines updated assessment of project.

### Step 5 — Manage information extraction

Don't reveal final assessment during meeting. Keep options open. Final assessment emerges from post-meeting analysis.

### Step 6 — Closing tactics

Leave on neutral or positive note even if assessment is negative. Burning bridges costs future information access. Polite skepticism > active confrontation.

### Step 7 — Post-meeting immediate notes

Within hour after meeting:
- What did they answer well?
- What did they evade?
- What was unexpected (positive or negative)?
- What new questions emerge?
- What's the updated assessment?

These feed `expert-debrief` command.

## Worked example — applying to FusionCorp meeting

### Opening:
"Thanks for the time. Before we get into specifics, can you walk me through the basic plasma physics of your approach? I want to make sure I understand what makes your tokamak different from ITER's approach."

This signals technical engagement without being adversarial. Lets them establish framing while you assess their fundamentals.

### Probing (deploy combat questions tactically):

Start with **economic question** (Q1 from combat list — capex anchor): less adversarial, gets them comfortable with specific numbers.

Move to **comparison question** (Q4 — Helion alternative): reveals their thinking about competitive landscape and risks.

Insert **marker question** mid-meeting (Q6 — bet against your own approach): catches them off guard.

Then deploy **failure mode questions** (Q2 tritium, Q3 materials, Q7 plasma stability): these are the decisive ones.

End probing with **maturity probe** (Q5 — timeline compression vs ITER): closes on aggressiveness of claims.

### Use silence and follow-ups throughout:

After each major answer, brief silence. If incomplete, follow up. If satisfactory, move to next.

### Reading the room:
Watch CEO Chen vs CTO Smith vs Advisor Williams interactions. Who speaks for what? Who looks at whom? Where is real expertise located?

### Closing:
"Thanks — what I take from this is [neutral summary]. The question I'd most want resolved before further discussion is [the most decisive failure mode question they answered weakest]."

Leaves you in position to follow up or not. Doesn't commit. Signals you're paying attention.

## Anti-patterns

- **Adversarial throughout.** Hostile questioning damages information flow. Polite probing extracts more.
- **Showing your hand.** If they realize you've identified specific weakness, they prepare counter-narratives for follow-up. Don't reveal what you know fully.
- **Asking checklist style.** Reading questions one by one mechanically. Should feel conversational; questions should connect to their answers.
- **Too aggressive marker question.** "Where would you bet against yourself?" works as middle question, not opening shot.
- **Not following up.** Initial answer is rarely complete. Follow-ups extract real content.
- **Revealing assessment in meeting.** Save final assessment for post-meeting analysis. Maintains options.
- **Not taking notes.** Meeting is dense; you'll miss things without notes. Visible note-taking signals engagement plus aids memory.

## Integration with Expert protocol

Tier 3 — invoked for:
- High-stakes meetings (significant investment, strategic decision)
- Owner inexperienced with the meeting style
- Complex multi-party meetings
- Adversarial settings (negotiation, due diligence)

Output is integrated into briefing as **separate "in-meeting playbook"** — owner reviews this last before meeting, has tactical reminders ready.

Stored in ExpertBriefing under `tacticalProtocol`.

Most powerful when used together with combat questions and pre-meeting intel — the three together turn briefing material into successful meeting performance.
