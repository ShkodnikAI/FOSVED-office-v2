# Honest Claims Discipline — Every Claim Must Be Provable

Marketing's reputation problem comes from one habit: saying things that aren't true, or true-ish, or unprovable. This skill is the floor. Every claim the department makes must be one a skeptic could check and confirm.

## Prerequisites

- Campaign or marketing material being created
- The actual facts about the product are known

## Core principle

> A marketing claim is a promise. If the promise can't be kept or can't be checked, making it is not persuasion — it's a debt that comes due when the customer discovers the gap. The cost of a broken promise is paid in trust, and trust does not refund. Say only what's true and provable; it's also more persuasive, because specificity beats superlatives.

## The verifiability test

Before any claim ships, ask: **could a skeptical person verify this?**

- "The fastest tool on the market" → can a skeptic verify? Only if there's a public, fair benchmark. Otherwise: unverifiable → cut it.
- "Saves users an average of 4 hours a week" → verifiable IF there's data behind the "4 hours". With data: keep. Without: cut or soften.
- "Used by over 2,000 businesses" → verifiable if true and countable. Keep.
- "The best decision you'll make this year" → unverifiable opinion dressed as fact → cut.

If a claim fails the test, it has two fates: back it with evidence, or remove it.

## Banned without proof (hard rule 2)

These words/claims are forbidden unless backed by specific, citable evidence:

- **"Best" / "#1" / "leading" / "top"** — requires a defined category and a fair measurement
- **"Guaranteed"** — requires an actual guarantee the business will honor
- **"Revolutionary" / "game-changing" / "breakthrough"** — almost never provable; usually empty
- **"Everyone is switching" / "join thousands"** — requires real numbers
- **"Proven"** — requires the actual proof
- **"Risk-free"** — requires it to genuinely be risk-free
- **Specific results** ("double your revenue") — requires evidence it's typical, not cherry-picked

The issue isn't the words themselves — it's claiming them without backing. "The #1 tool by independent benchmark X" is fine. "The #1 tool" alone is not.

## Specificity beats superlatives

The honest path is also the more effective one. Vague superlatives are weak; specific facts are strong:

- Weak: "Incredibly fast." → Strong: "Generates the report in under 3 seconds."
- Weak: "Loved by users." → Strong: "1,800 businesses renewed last year."
- Weak: "Revolutionary approach." → Strong: "Replaces the 5 separate tools you currently use."
- Weak: "Best-in-class support." → Strong: "Median support response time: 12 minutes."

A skeptic relaxes when given a checkable fact. A skeptic tenses when given a superlative. Specificity is both honest and persuasive — there's no trade-off.

## Banned manipulative tactics (hard rule 6)

Beyond false claims, certain *true-but-manipulative* tactics are banned:

- **False urgency** — "Only 3 left!" / "Offer ends in 1 hour!" when it isn't true. Fake scarcity.
- **Fear-mongering** — manufacturing anxiety about a problem to sell the solution. Inflating a real fear is still manipulation.
- **Targeting vulnerable groups** — children, people in crisis, people with addictions or compulsions, the financially desperate. Marketing influences behavior; aiming that influence at someone who can't defend against it is harm.
- **Dark patterns** — confusing opt-outs, hidden costs, pre-checked boxes, "confirmshaming" ("No, I don't want to save money").
- **Bait and switch** — advertising one thing, delivering another.

Genuine urgency (a real deadline) and genuine scarcity (a real limit) are fine — state them honestly. The ban is on *manufactured* pressure.

## Why this is practical, not just ethical

This isn't only a moral stance. False/manipulative marketing is bad business:

- **Trust doesn't refund.** A customer who feels deceived doesn't just leave — they warn others. The damage compounds.
- **Legal exposure.** False advertising is regulated. Unverifiable superlatives and fake scarcity can bring real penalties.
- **Churn.** Manipulation gets the first conversion and loses the relationship. The customer acquired by a false promise churns fast and the CAC is wasted.
- **Word of mouth inverts.** Honest marketing earns referrals. Deceptive marketing earns warnings.

The honest campaign has worse vanity metrics sometimes and better real metrics almost always.

## The procedure

### Step 1 — List every claim
Extract each factual or evaluative statement from the material. "Fast", "trusted", "saves time", "best for X" — every one.

### Step 2 — Test each
For each claim: verifiable? If yes — is the evidence actually in hand? If no — cut or soften.

### Step 3 — Replace superlatives with specifics
Where a superlative survived, try to replace it with the specific fact underneath. "Fast" → the actual number.

### Step 4 — Scan for manipulation
Check the material against the banned-tactics list. Fake urgency, fear, vulnerable targeting, dark patterns — remove.

### Step 5 — Record the check
Mark `claimsVerified` on the campaign with a note on what was checked. A campaign can't launch without this.

## Worked example

Draft ad copy for the Fosved miniapp:

**Draft (before discipline):**
"The revolutionary #1 productivity tool that guarantees you'll never lose track of your business again. Thousands of smart owners have already switched. Limited spots — don't miss out!"

Run the test:
- "revolutionary" — unverifiable → cut
- "#1" — no benchmark → cut
- "guarantees you'll never lose track" — can't be guaranteed → cut
- "thousands have switched" — is it true and countable? if not → cut
- "limited spots — don't miss out" — is it really limited? if not, fake urgency → cut

**Rewritten (after discipline):**
"See the whole state of your business in one place. The Fosved miniapp pulls your scattered notes, numbers, and updates into a single view — so decisions stop feeling like guesswork. Built for owners running things solo."

Every sentence is now checkable: it does pull information into one view; it is built for solo owners. No superlatives, no fake scarcity. It's also sharper — it names the specific pain (scattered information, guesswork) instead of shouting "revolutionary".

## Anti-patterns

- **Superlatives as filler.** "Best", "leading", "revolutionary" sprinkled in because they sound like marketing. They sound like *bad* marketing.
- **Unbacked specific claims.** "Saves 4 hours a week" with no data behind the 4.
- **Cherry-picked results presented as typical.** One customer doubled revenue; the ad implies everyone will.
- **Fake urgency / scarcity.** Countdown timers that reset, "only X left" that never runs out.
- **Fear-based selling.** Manufacturing or inflating anxiety to drive the sale.
- **Targeting the vulnerable.** Aiming persuasion at children, people in crisis, the desperate.
- **Dark patterns.** Confusing opt-outs, confirmshaming, hidden costs.
- **"Everyone knows" claims.** "Everyone is switching" — unverifiable social proof.
- **Skipping the claims check.** Launching without verifying every claim is provable.

## When NOT to soften

Honesty doesn't mean timidity. If a claim is *true and provable*, state it boldly. "1,800 businesses renewed last year" — if true, say it confidently. The discipline removes false claims, not strong ones. A real, specific, verified strength deserves a clear, confident statement.

```
Claim 1: "<exact claim>"
  Verifiable? yes / no
  Evidence: <what backs it / "none">
  Verdict: keep | replace with specific | cut
  [if replaced] New form: "<specific version>"

[repeat for every claim]

MANIPULATION SCAN
- Fake urgency/scarcity: none found / <found, removed>
- Fear-based framing: none found / <found, removed>
- Vulnerable-group targeting: none found / <found, removed>
- Dark patterns: none found / <found, removed>

RESULT: claims verified — campaign cleared to launch | claims failed — fixes required
```

This sets `claimsVerified` on the `MarketingCampaign`.
