# Competing Alternatives — The Other Solutions To The Same Problem

When attention focuses on a single emerging technology, the multiple competing solutions to the same underlying problem are often overlooked. The narrative settles on Technology A as "the future"; meanwhile Technologies B, C, and D are pursuing the same goal through different approaches.

History often produces surprising winners. Mainstream attention focused on viral-vector vaccines while mRNA was viewed as exotic; mRNA won. Hard drive incumbents focused on improving existing magnetic technology while solid-state storage was building separately; solid-state increasingly wins. Many lithium-ion improvements were sought while solid-state batteries were funded as long-shot; solid-state may yet win.

This skill is the discipline of systematically considering alternatives. For every prominent technology, what else is being developed to solve the same problem? Which alternative might displace it?

## Prerequisites

- Technology under analysis with `disruption-scoring`
- Awareness that current dominant technology is not always future winner
- Time investment for parallel analysis of multiple options

## Core principle

> Disruption analysis focused on single technology misses the disruption from alternative approach. The same underlying problem may be solved multiple ways; the historically dominant solution often loses to alternatives that initially seem inferior. Systematic consideration of competing alternatives prevents this analytical blind spot.

## Why alternatives matter

Three reasons:

**1. Innovation often comes from unexpected directions.**
Existing dominant technology improves incrementally. New approach can leapfrog through structural advantages.

**2. Competing approaches reveal underlying problem structure.**
Comparing alternatives clarifies what the actual constraints are vs which constraints are specific to current approach.

**3. Investment hedge.**
If multiple approaches are viable, single-approach investment is concentrated. Diversified across approaches captures whichever wins.

## The procedure

### Step 1 — Identify underlying problem

Before considering technologies, identify the underlying problem they're solving:
- "Solid-state batteries" → underlying problem: "denser, safer, cheaper energy storage"
- "Generative AI" → underlying problem: "automated content generation and reasoning"
- "mRNA vaccines" → underlying problem: "rapid, adaptable immune system training"

The underlying problem level is where competition exists. Technologies are alternative solutions to the same underlying problem.

### Step 2 — Catalog competing approaches

For the underlying problem, identify all serious approaches being pursued:
- Mainstream approach (current dominant)
- Improvement of mainstream (incremental)
- Alternative approaches (different paradigm)
- Combinations and hybrids

Be inclusive at first — narrow later.

### Step 3 — Score each approach

For each alternative, apply standard scoring:
- Hype cycle stage
- Disruption probability
- Technology OOB position
- Time horizon to commercialization

Rank approaches by likelihood of becoming dominant solution.

### Step 4 — Identify decisive variables

What variables determine which approach wins?

Common decisive variables:
- Cost trajectory of each approach
- Performance ceiling of each approach
- Manufacturability of each approach
- Regulatory pathway differences
- Network effects favoring one approach
- Path-dependent advantages

Often one or two variables are decisive; others are minor.

### Step 5 — Forecast convergence

Will multiple approaches coexist or will one dominate?

Outcomes:
- **Single winner:** one approach dominates; others decline
- **Application split:** different approaches dominate different applications
- **Hybrid solution:** combinations of approaches win
- **Co-existence:** multiple approaches viable simultaneously

For each outcome, what's the probability and timeline?

### Step 6 — Identify investment implications

Different outcomes produce different investment positioning:

**Single winner:** concentrated bet on most likely winner; risk of being wrong about identity
**Application split:** diversified across applications
**Hybrid:** position in components used by multiple approaches
**Co-existence:** broader exposure across approaches

### Step 7 — Identify upset risks

For analyses concluding one approach will likely dominate, what could upset that conclusion?
- Specific technical breakthrough in alternative approach
- Regulatory shift favoring different approach
- Cost trajectory change
- Manufacturing breakthrough enabling different approach

### Step 8 — Output

Competing alternatives analysis with implications.

## Worked example — battery technology beyond lithium-ion

**Underlying problem:** denser, safer, cheaper energy storage for electrification.

**Competing approaches:**

**1. Lithium-ion improvements (incremental)**
- Higher-density chemistries (NCA, NMC variations)
- Process improvements
- Hype cycle: Plateau (mature)
- Disruption probability vs current LFP: low (incremental)
- Timeline: continuous improvement
- Beneficiaries: established battery makers (Panasonic, LG, CATL, etc.)

**2. Solid-state batteries**
- Replace liquid electrolyte with solid
- Substantially higher density potential (2-3x)
- Hype cycle: late Trough / early Slope
- Disruption probability: high if cost reduction continues
- Timeline: 5-10 years for mass deployment
- Beneficiaries: pioneers (QuantumScape, Solid Power, others), partners (Toyota, VW)

**3. Sodium-ion batteries**
- Lower density but cheaper raw materials
- Hype cycle: Slope (commercializing)
- Disruption probability: high for grid storage / low-cost vehicles
- Timeline: 1-3 years for grid; 3-7 years for transportation
- Beneficiaries: Chinese battery makers (CATL specifically), startup pioneers

**4. Lithium-sulfur**
- Theoretical density advantages
- Cycle life challenges
- Hype cycle: still in Trough
- Disruption probability: medium uncertain
- Timeline: 7-15 years if challenges resolved

**5. Flow batteries (vanadium, others)**
- For grid-scale specifically
- Different application than transportation
- Hype cycle: Slope for specific applications
- Disruption probability: high for grid applications

**Decisive variables:**
- Cost trajectory: solid-state must achieve cost parity with LFP for transportation; sodium-ion already approaching for some applications
- Performance ceiling: lithium-ion improvements approaching theoretical limits; solid-state has substantial headroom
- Manufacturing scaling: solid-state manufacturing not yet proven at scale
- Regulatory environment: battery safety requirements favor solid-state

**Convergence forecast:**
- 5 years: lithium-ion improvements continue dominant; solid-state demonstrating but not mass; sodium-ion taking grid + low-end transport
- 10 years: solid-state substantial in premium transport; sodium-ion dominant in grid + low-cost transport; lithium-ion mid-market
- 15 years: solid-state likely dominant new transportation; multiple chemistries coexist by application

**Application split outcome most likely.**

**Investment implications:**
- Diversified exposure across chemistries
- Specific bets on solid-state pioneers (asymmetric upside if win)
- Specific bets on sodium-ion leaders (CATL, others)
- Established lithium-ion players retain market share but margin pressure

**Upset risks:**
- Solid-state cost reduction dramatically faster than expected → solid-state wins everything sooner
- Sodium-ion failures in scaling → lithium-ion retains low end longer
- New approach (lithium-sulfur breakthrough or unforeseen) → portfolio exposure required

## Anti-patterns

- **Single-approach focus.** Most analyses focus on dominant approach; alternatives ignored. Discipline against this is what this skill provides.
- **Underestimating incumbents.** Existing technology often improves more than expected. "Lithium-ion is finished" was claim 5 years ago; lithium-ion still improving substantially.
- **Overestimating outsiders.** Conversely, alternative approaches face real challenges. "Solid-state will dominate" was claim 5 years ago; still not commercially available at scale.
- **Treating approaches as binary.** Often hybrids win. "Either A or B" misses "both, integrated" possibility.
- **Ignoring application differentiation.** Same problem solved differently by application. Battery for car vs grid vs phone may have different winning approaches.
- **Decisive variables blindness.** Not identifying which variables determine outcome. Sometimes obvious; sometimes not.
