# Leaked Roadmap Analysis — Reading What Was Not Meant For You

Companies maintain detailed internal documents about strategic direction — product roadmaps, R&D priorities, competitive analyses, investment plans. These documents reveal real strategy more accurately than public communications. Sometimes they leak.

Leaks come from disgruntled employees, regulatory disclosures, court proceedings, journalistic investigations, hostile actors, accidental publication. When they appear and are authentic, they provide highest-quality intelligence available — direct view of how a company sees itself and its plans.

This is Tier 3 because authentic leaks are rare, analysis is delicate, and misuse risks credibility. When applicable, however, this skill produces intelligence other approaches cannot match.

## Prerequisites

- Access to leak source (publicly available leaked documents, regulatory disclosures, court records)
- Time investment for careful authentication
- Discipline against treating leaks credulously

## Core principle

> Authentic internal documents reveal strategic direction more accurately than public communications. But many "leaks" are inauthentic, manipulated, or selectively released. The discipline is rigorous authentication before analysis, then careful extraction of intelligence accounting for the leak's timing, source, and possible motivations.

## Authentication discipline

Before any analysis, authenticate the document:

**Source check:**
- Where did the document come from?
- What's the source's credibility track record?
- Are there indications of intermediation that could have altered content?

**Internal consistency check:**
- Does the document use authentic-looking corporate language?
- Are details consistent with known facts?
- Are there anachronisms or factual errors that wouldn't exist in authentic version?

**External consistency check:**
- Are the claims in the document consistent with other observable facts (talent flow, patents, public statements)?
- Are there subsequent corporate actions that confirm or contradict the document?
- Have the named individuals confirmed or denied?

**Motivation check:**
- Who benefits from this document being released?
- Is the timing of the leak consistent with strategic motivation?
- Could the document be intentionally crafted disinformation?

If authentication fails or is inconclusive, treat document as unreliable and apply standard low-confidence labels.

## Types of authentic leaks

**Disgruntled employee leaks:**
- Often substantively accurate but selectively negative
- May omit positive context
- Timing often follows departure or termination
- Reliability: high for facts presented; biased in selection

**Regulatory disclosure leaks:**
- Required disclosures sometimes contain more than companies intended
- Sometimes intentionally redacted documents released through FOIA, court orders
- Reliability: very high — these are forced disclosures

**Whistleblower releases:**
- Often through journalistic intermediaries
- May include context and selection by journalist
- Generally authenticated by intermediary
- Reliability: typically high; verify intermediary's track record

**Hacked documents:**
- Released by hackers (may be state-sponsored)
- Authenticity often verified through cryptographic analysis
- Selection possibly motivated
- Reliability: facts often accurate; selection often political

**Court proceedings:**
- Documents introduced as evidence become public
- Highest reliability when evidence is authenticated for court use
- Often partial — only documents related to litigation

**Conference / off-record statements:**
- Sometimes published despite request not to be
- Reliability: variable depending on attendee credibility

## The procedure

### Step 1 — Receive document

Document obtained through one of the source types above. Note source explicitly.

### Step 2 — Authenticate

Apply authentication discipline:
- Source check
- Internal consistency
- External consistency
- Motivation check

If authentication clearly fails: document discarded, analysis ends.
If authentication clearly succeeds: proceed to extraction.
If authentication ambiguous: proceed but with reduced confidence labels throughout.

### Step 3 — Date the document

When was the document created? Strategic information has time-decay. A 2024 roadmap is much less valuable than a 2026 roadmap for current decisions.

Look for:
- Timestamps
- References to known events
- Software metadata (where preserved)
- Linguistic dating (corporate jargon evolves)

### Step 4 — Identify document type and scope

What kind of document?
- Top-level roadmap (highest value)
- Engineering specification (technical detail)
- Strategic analysis (competitive insights)
- Financial planning (investment direction)
- Personnel-related (talent strategy)

What scope?
- Full company
- Specific division
- Specific project

Different types yield different intelligence.

### Step 5 — Extract intelligence

Read document for:
- Stated strategic priorities
- Specific projects and timelines
- Competitive positioning analysis
- Technology direction
- Personnel and resource allocation
- Anticipated obstacles or challenges

Take careful notes; document is reference material, not summary.

### Step 6 — Cross-validate against public information

For each major finding from the document, check against:
- Public statements (consistent or divergent?)
- Patent activity (does document explain patent direction?)
- Talent flow (does document explain hiring patterns?)
- Investment patterns (does document explain capital deployment?)

Document increases value when it explains otherwise-puzzling public observations.

### Step 7 — Identify aged intelligence

Some content from older documents may have been updated or superseded:
- Plans that haven't materialized may have been replaced
- Personnel changes may have shifted priorities
- Technical approaches may have evolved

Mark intelligence with age: contemporary, somewhat dated, likely superseded.

### Step 8 — Generate intelligence findings

What does document reveal that public information doesn't?
- Strategic priorities not publicly disclosed
- Specific timelines not publicly committed
- Competitive perceptions of company about rivals
- Anticipated regulatory or competitive challenges
- Resource commitments

### Step 9 — Output

Carefully labeled intelligence summary. Output emphasizes:
- What is reliably learned
- What is uncertain
- What's likely time-decayed

## Worked example — hypothetical leaked AI lab roadmap

**Source:** purportedly leaked product roadmap from major AI lab, published by tech journalism outlet.

**Authentication:**
- Source: established journalism outlet, multiple cross-checks, named source citing
- Internal consistency: corporate language authentic; technical details consistent with public capabilities
- External consistency: subsequent product launches matched leaked timeline; talent moves matched
- Motivation: leak appeared to come from departing employee with mixed motivations; strategic intelligence content seemed unfiltered
- Result: authenticated with high confidence

**Document date:** approximately 6 months prior to leak

**Document type:** product roadmap covering 12-24 months

**Scope:** primary commercial AI products

**Key intelligence extracted:**
- Specific product launches with months/quarters
- Technical capability targets (parameter counts, training compute, specific benchmarks)
- Pricing strategy direction
- Specific competitive positioning vs named rivals
- Anticipated market opportunities and timing
- Resource constraints (compute capacity, specific talent gaps)

**Cross-validation:**
- Public statements: largely consistent but more aggressive privately
- Patent activity: matches; explains some otherwise-puzzling filings
- Talent flow: explains specific recent hires
- Investment: matches reported R&D spending patterns

**Findings:**
- Lab more aggressive on commercial scaling than public messaging suggests
- Specific timeline for major product launch earlier than industry consensus
- Lab views specific competitor (named) as primary threat
- Compute capacity constraint explicitly acknowledged

**Implications for ОСП:**
- Investment in lab (or parent company) supported by intelligence
- Specific competitor (named) positioned for response
- Compute infrastructure providers (NVIDIA, others) benefit from acknowledged constraint
- Specific industries lab targets first (named in document) face transformation pressure earlier

**Confidence and aging:**
- Overall intelligence: high confidence due to authentication
- Specific timeline: moderate (6-month aged; specifics may have shifted)
- Strategic direction: high (less likely to have shifted)
- Competitive positioning: high (durable insight)

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating all leaks as authentic.** Many leaks are fake, manipulated, or partially authentic. Authentication required.
- **Treating leaks as gospel.** Even authentic leaks reflect time-bound thinking. Aged intelligence partially superseded.
- **Selection bias awareness failure.** Whoever leaked chose what to leak. What's NOT in the leak may be as important.
- **Ignoring leaker motivations.** Leakers have reasons. Sometimes those reasons distort what's shared.
- **Public discussion of recently-leaked content.** Public spreading of leak details can compromise sources, invite legal action, or signal access. Use intelligence privately for analysis.
- **Confirmation bias enabling.** Leaks confirming prior beliefs may receive less skeptical authentication. Apply same authentication regardless.
- **Forgetting time decay.** Older leaks become less valuable. Discount appropriately.

## Integration with Лаборатория знаний

Tier 3 — invoked rarely, only when:
- Authenticated leak appears in domain of interest
- Public information insufficient for decisive intelligence
- Owner-approved use of escalated intelligence sources

**Output:** carefully labeled, integrated into KnowledgeArtifact with explicit sourcing and authentication notes.

This skill applied carefully provides intelligence that other approaches cannot. Applied carelessly, it risks credibility through bad authentication or strategic compromise through inappropriate disclosure. Discipline distinguishes the two outcomes.
